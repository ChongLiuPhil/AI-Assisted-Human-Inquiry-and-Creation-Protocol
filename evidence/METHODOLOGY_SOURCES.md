## 1. ICMJE — authorship, final approval, accountability

**Source:** International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, *Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors*.

https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html

**Verified use in article:**

- authorship is connected to responsibility/accountability for published work;
- authorship criteria include final approval of the version to be published;
- authors agree to be accountable for the accuracy/integrity of the work and to ensure problems are appropriately investigated/resolved.

**Status:** `EVIDENCE-CONSTRAINT`.

**Re-check before submission:** yes. Authorship recommendations can change.

---

## 2. Springer Nature / Nature — AI use and human accountability

**Source:** Springer Nature / Nature editorial policy pages on Artificial Intelligence and authorship.

Examples checked:

- https://www.nature.com/natcatal/editorial-policies/ai
- https://www.nature.com/npjai/editorial-policies/authorship

**Verified use in article:**

- AI/LLM systems are not treated as authors under current policies because authorship entails accountability;
- human scholarly judgment and accountability remain human responsibilities;
- AI use may require disclosure depending on context and use.

**Status:** `EVIDENCE-CONSTRAINT`.

**Important qualification:** policies vary by journal/publisher and change over time. HARC must not present one publisher's policy as a universal law of scholarly authorship.

**Re-check before submission:** yes.

## 3. CRediT — contributor-role transparency

**Source:** NISO CRediT Contributor Role Taxonomy.

https://credit.niso.org/

**Verified use in article:**

- CRediT defines 14 contributor roles;
- it is designed to improve transparency about contributions;
- the taxonomy is not itself a rule for determining who qualifies for authorship.

**Status:** `EVIDENCE-CONSTRAINT`.

## 4. UNESCO — human-centred generative-AI governance

**Source:** UNESCO, *Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research*.

https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research

**Verified use in article:**

- UNESCO frames generative-AI governance in education/research through a human-centred approach;
- the guidance emphasizes policy, privacy, ethical validation, and meaningful human use.

**Status:** background policy context, not a direct source for HARC's specific responsibility model.

## 5. Clark & Chalmers (1998) — external cognitive resources

**Reference:** Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. (1998). “The Extended Mind.” *Analysis*, 58(1), 7–19.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/analys/58.1.7

**Use in article:** conceptual comparison for understanding external resources as parts of cognitive processes under some conditions.

**Qualification:** HARC does not require acceptance of the strong extended-mind thesis. The repository is described more modestly as an external cognitive scaffold / persistent research-state substrate.

## 6. Hutchins (1995) — distributed cognition

**Reference:** Hutchins, E. (1995). *Cognition in the Wild*. MIT Press.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/1881.001.0001

**Use in article:** conceptual background for cognition distributed across people, tools, representations, and organized activity.

**Qualification:** the article uses this as an analogy and methodological resource, not as proof that HARC repositories are autonomous cognitive agents.

## 7. Hardwig (1985) — epistemic dependence

**Reference:** Hardwig, J. (1985). “Epistemic Dependence.” *The Journal of Philosophy*, 82(7), 335–349.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523

**Use in article:** philosophical background for the idea that knowledge practices already involve dependence on other epistemic agents and expert authority.

**Qualification:** AI systems are not simply equivalent to human experts; HARC uses the literature to frame dependence as a governance problem rather than to assume equivalence.

---

## 8. Parasuraman & Riley (1997) — automation reliance

**Reference:** Parasuraman, R., & Riley, V. (1997). “Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse.” *Human Factors*, 39(2).

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1518/001872097778543886

**Use in article:** background for risks of automation misuse/over-reliance and failures of monitoring.

**Qualification:** the paper predates modern generative AI; it is used for the general automation-reliance problem, not as direct empirical evidence about LLM use.

# Claims that remain HARC proposals rather than externally established facts

The following should NOT be written as if established by the above literature merely because they are central to HARC:

- that framework approval is the best general solution to AI-era authorship;
- that “responsibility concentration” is the correct universal model of human responsibility;
- that semantic version control necessarily reduces semantic drift;
- that a repository-centered workflow measurably improves research integrity;
- that HARC reduces human review burden without increasing serious error.

These are protocol hypotheses / normative proposals that require argument and, where possible, empirical testing.
