## 1. ICMJE — authorship, final approval, accountability

**Primary source:** International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, *Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors*.

https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html

**Reverified 2026-09-17.**

### Verified claims

ICMJE currently states that authorship criteria include:

- substantial contribution to conception/design or acquisition/analysis/interpretation of data;
- drafting or critical review for important intellectual content;
- **final approval of the version to be published**;
- agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work, including appropriate investigation/resolution of questions related to accuracy or integrity.

ICMJE also states that AI-assisted technology use should be disclosed and that authors remain responsible for submitted material.

### Permitted use in HARC article

Use ICMJE as a concrete example showing that at least one influential scholarly authorship framework connects authorship with final approval and accountability.

### Do not overclaim

Do **not** present ICMJE as a universal authorship law across all disciplines or venues.

**Status:** `EVIDENCE-CONSTRAINT`.

**Re-check before submission:** yes.

## 2. Nature Portfolio / Springer Nature — AI use and human accountability

**Primary policy hub:** Nature Portfolio Editorial Policies.

https://www.nature.com/nature-portfolio/editorial-policies

**Current AI policy examples checked 2026-09-17:**

- Nature Portfolio journal AI-policy pages, including Scientific Reports AI policy;
- Nature Methods editorial, *Using AI responsibly in scientific publishing* (2026), DOI: 10.1038/s41592-026-03020-1.

### Verified claims

Current Nature Portfolio policy materials state, in substance, that:

- authors remain accountable for originality, accuracy, and integrity;
- AI use should be disclosed where required for transparency;
- AI tools cannot assume the accountability required of human authors;
- scholarly/editorial judgment remains a human responsibility in the relevant roles;
- policies are expected to evolve and should be re-checked before submission.

### Permitted use in HARC article

Use Nature Portfolio as another current example of human accountability remaining central in AI-assisted scholarly publishing.

### Do not overclaim

Do not write that all publishers use identical AI rules. Nature Portfolio policy is evidence of one major publisher's current framework, not a universal norm.

**Status:** `EVIDENCE-CONSTRAINT`.

**Re-check before submission:** yes.

## 3. CRediT — contributor-role transparency

**Primary source:** NISO CRediT Contributor Role Taxonomy.

https://credit.niso.org/

**Standard:** ANSI/NISO Z39.104-2022, DOI: 10.3789/ansi.niso.z39.104-2022.

**Reverified 2026-09-17.**

### Verified claims

- CRediT defines **14 contributor roles**.
- It is intended to represent contributions to scholarly outputs in a transparent, structured way.
- It complements rather than by itself determines authorship status.

### Permitted use in HARC article

Use CRediT to distinguish two questions:

1. who performed which kinds of work;
2. who satisfies a venue's authorship/accountability requirements.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523

**Bibliographic metadata reverified 2026-09-17.**

### Use in article

Philosophical background for the idea that knowledge practices can involve dependence on others' epistemic authority and expertise.

## 8. Parasuraman & Riley (1997) — automation reliance

**Reference:** Parasuraman, R., & Riley, V. (1997). “Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse.” *Human Factors*, 39(2), 230–253.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1518/001872097778543886

**Bibliographic metadata and abstract reverified 2026-09-17.**

### Verified relevance

The article explicitly treats automation **misuse** as over-reliance that can produce monitoring failures or decision biases.

### Qualification

The paper predates modern generative AI. It supports the general automation-reliance analogy, not direct empirical claims about LLM-assisted research.

# Claims that remain HARC proposals rather than externally established facts

The following MUST NOT be written as if established by the above literature merely because they are central to HARC:

- that framework approval is the best general solution to AI-era authorship;
- that “responsibility concentration” is the correct universal model of human responsibility;
- that “generation–verification asymmetry” is an established technical term;
- that “semantic version control” is an established scholarly methodology term in HARC's sense;
- that a repository-centered workflow measurably reduces semantic drift;
- that HARC improves research integrity;
- that HARC reduces human review burden without increasing serious error;
- that a human-approved framework alone is sufficient for authorship under any external venue rule.

These are HARC hypotheses, proposed concepts, or normative claims that require argument and, where possible, empirical testing.
